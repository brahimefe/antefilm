Exodus 1960

Directed by Otto Preminger, from Leon Uris's novel; screenplay by Dalton Trumbo. Holocaust survivors smuggled out of the British camps on Cyprus in 1947, and the founding of a state.

Two of the dead, a Jewish girl and an Arab friend, are laid in one grave. Over it, the hero swears that the two peoples will one day share the land in life as they now share it in death. The film can picture a common future only over a common grave.

The five questions

How the film answers

Promise

Yes

Does the film show a future in which the conflict is over?

The whole film moves towards the UN partition vote and the promise of a state. The future is certain, and it is good; the only open question is how fast it arrives.

Whose future

Partly

Is there a place in that future for the other side?

The closing oath promises a shared land, and Taha, the Arab friend, is drawn with warmth. But Arabs enter the future only as friends who have earned a place in it. The people of Taha's village quietly vanish from the story.

To check while watching: how the village's emptying is shown, and who is said to have caused it.

Belief

Yes

Do the characters believe the promise can be kept?

No one doubts the destination. The argument is only about means: the diplomacy of the Haganah against the bombs of the Irgun. Even that argument ends in reconciliation.

Cost

Partly

Is the price of the promise shown, and who pays it?

The price is real: Akiva, Karen and Taha die. But it is told as the sacrifice of one's own side. The losses of the other side do not appear as losses.

Past

Yes

When the future grows uncertain, does the film draw its legitimacy from the Holocaust?

The refugees on the ship are survivors, and Dov's confession of what was done to him in Auschwitz is one of the film's turning points. The past is told to justify the future. Here, though, the future is not uncertain: memory and promise work side by side.

To check while watching: the timecode and wording of Dov's confession.

For the hypothesis

In 1960 both lanes read yes. Memory does not replace the promise here; it props it up. So the question for later films is sharper: when the promise withdraws, is memory left standing on its own?
