IConstruction

What is built as problem, what is left as given

Camera recordA revolution in Iran is staged in the opening as onset: storyboards trace the Shah, the embassy seizure, the hostages. The cause is glossed in a minute of voiceover; what remains is the standing problem of Americans trapped abroad. The CIA, in the figure of Tony Mendez, supplies the agency the situation lacks.
Ground recordThe film's prologue compresses 1953 to 1979 — the CIA-MI6 coup against Mosaddegh, the Shah's installation, SAVAK — into context. The standing problem ceases to be a US action and becomes an Iranian state. The ground record of US covert action in Iran, which the CIA formally acknowledged in documents released in 2013, does not return to the frame.

Sources

  1. National Security Archive (2013). CIA Confirms Role in 1953 Iran Coup. Electronic Briefing Book No. 435, 19 August 2013.
  2. US Department of State (2017). Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Iran, 1951–1954. Washington, DC: Office of the Historian.

Selection note

Considered: US arms sales to the Shah, 1970s. Not filed: the 1953 coup is the construction the prologue itself compresses.

IIAddress

Who stands in for the species

Camera recordSix American diplomats hidden in the Canadian ambassador's residence. The film addresses an audience for whom their safety is the stake, their faces the ones the camera holds. The fifty-two hostages at the embassy itself remain peripheral; the Iranians who appear are mostly the crowd, mostly hostile, mostly without name.
Ground recordThe students who took the embassy had their own demands — the Shah's return to stand trial among them — and later published their own account of the seizure. The film records none of it. The exception ("rescue") requires an unspecified mass ("hostility") to function.

Sources

  1. Ebtekar, M. (2000). Takeover in Tehran: The Inside Story of the 1979 U.S. Embassy Capture (as told to Fred A. Reed). Vancouver: Talonbooks.

Selection note

Considered: Iranian casualty figures of the revolution. Not filed: the attention concerns who is given a voice, and the hostage-takers' own account is the direct counter-record.

IIIExclusion

Which carriers of the same keyword stay off-record

Camera recordThe word rescue structures the film. The CIA rescues six. The audience is invited to feel the rescue as the resolution of the crisis. The closing intertitles record the diplomats' safe return as ending.
Ground recordThe same word carries another file. Rescue in US foreign policy 1953–2012 includes the deposition of elected governments framed as rescue from communism; the 1980 Operation Eagle Claw, which left eight US servicemen dead and is absent from the film; and the rescues that did not happen — embassy workers, civilians under sanctions, populations under US-backed regimes.

Sources

  1. US Department of State (2017). Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Iran, 1951–1954.
  2. Special Operations Review Group (1980). Rescue Mission Report [the Holloway Report]. Washington, DC: Joint Chiefs of Staff.

Selection note

Exclusion may range historically. Eagle Claw is filed because it is the same crisis's failed rescue, omitted by the film.

IVPrescription

What the framing proposes as future-reality

Camera recordThe remedy is exception. Where ordinary diplomacy fails, the cinematic ruse — a fake film crew — succeeds. The film proposes covert improvisation as the morally available form of action, with the CIA officer as its hero.
Ground recordThe same prescription operates at policy level: the doctrine of exceptional action — covert operation, special forces, targeted improvisation — as the legitimate modality of US foreign engagement, recurring from 1953 through 2012's drone-strike escalations.

Sources

  1. Mazzetti, M. (2013). The Way of the Knife: The CIA, a Secret Army, and a War at the Ends of the Earth. New York: Penguin Press.

Selection note

Interpretive pairing; a primary document on drone-strike policy in 2012 would strengthen it.

VCirculation

The infrastructure that pre-clears the framing

rescue exceptionalism

The political imagination in which the camera record — covert rescue as moral resolution, the exceptional act as legitimate intervention, the CIA officer as ethical agent — coincides with the ground record: a doctrine of exception that organises US foreign action across the period, from coup to covert operation to targeted strike. The rescue addresses some bodies as worth saving; the same imagination, on the ground, structures whom it is permissible to leave outside the rescue.
